Community governance is the system that makes responsibility visible. It explains why the community exists, who belongs, which conduct is expected, who can make decisions and how those decisions are reviewed.

Without governance, a platform accumulates informal rules. Experienced members learn them through conflict while newcomers remain uncertain. Administrators gain broad power without clear accountability. Technology can conceal this condition for a while, but it cannot resolve it.

## Write a community charter

A charter is a short operating foundation. It should state:

- the community purpose;
- who is eligible and how membership changes;
- the main activities and information available;
- what falls outside the community's scope;
- who operates and funds the space;
- the relationship to formal organisational processes;
- where members can ask questions or raise concerns.

Keep the public version readable. Detailed internal procedures can sit behind it, but members need the essentials before they participate.

Purpose provides the standard for later decisions. A proposed data field, integration or new group should support the charter rather than expand the portal by habit.

## Map decision rights

List consequential decisions: admitting or removing members, publishing official information, creating groups, changing standards, handling reports, approving integrations and exporting data.

Assign a responsible role and escalation path. Avoid broad labels such as “admin” when several teams share different duties. The software permissions should reflect the map as closely as practical.

Decide how absences and conflicts are handled. A moderator involved in a dispute may need another person to review it. A small community still needs a route when the usual administrator is unavailable.

## Design membership governance

Membership has a lifecycle: invitation, verification, onboarding, role change, inactivity, departure and possible return. Each stage affects privacy and security.

Use an authoritative eligibility source where one exists. Collect only the identity information required. Tell members which profile details are visible to peers and which are restricted to administrators.

Departure needs a policy. Remove access promptly when eligibility ends. Explain the treatment of past contributions and required records. Review groups and privileged roles regularly rather than relying on memory.

## Create community standards

Standards should help reasonable people understand expected conduct. Address respectful participation, privacy, confidentiality, harmful content, commercial activity, intellectual property and sector-specific concerns.

Use examples carefully. Overly vague rules create arbitrary enforcement; exhaustive lists encourage people to search for loopholes. State the principle and representative situations.

Make standards accessible at joining and where people contribute. Communicate material changes. Consider a summary in plain language with the complete version linked.

## Build a moderation system

Moderation combines people, process and tools. Define how members report, who reviews, what evidence is available, how severity is assessed and which responses are possible.

Consequences should be proportionate. A mistaken off-topic post, sustained harassment and a credible threat do not require the same response. Urgent safety issues may need external escalation beyond the portal.

Consider notice and review. Not every decision can be fully disclosed without harming privacy, but members should understand the process and have a route to challenge serious mistakes where appropriate.

Support moderators. They may encounter distressing content or conflict. Provide training, access to specialist advice and the authority to pause rather than demanding instant judgement.

## Separate official and community content

Members need to recognise what represents the organisation. Use distinct publishing roles and visual treatment for announcements, policies and service information.

Staff participation can create ambiguity. Set expectations about when a staff member is providing official advice, contributing professional experience or speaking personally. Important decisions should move into an authoritative format rather than remain in a comment.

## Govern information

Assign owners to maintained documents and guides. Include review dates. Archive or correct outdated material. Define which discussions become organisational records and how that capture occurs.

Retention should vary by purpose. Avoid both uncontrolled deletion and universal indefinite storage. Work with legal, privacy and records specialists where obligations apply.

Export and portability need governance too. Decide who can request an export, what approvals apply, how member privacy is protected and what context must accompany the data.

## Measure what helps a decision

Governance reviews can consider member feedback, unresolved reports, recurring support needs, inaccessible content, administrator access and whether the purpose remains valid.

Avoid making activity the primary success measure. Quiet members may still receive value. Highly active discussion can indicate confusion or conflict rather than health.

Collect the least detailed data needed for the review. Combine quantitative signals with member research and moderator experience.

## Review on a rhythm

Set a regular governance review and a trigger for material change. Consider:

- whether the charter still describes the community;
- membership and privileged access;
- unresolved or recurring moderation issues;
- current processors and integrations;
- retention and inactive content;
- accessibility barriers;
- member feedback and support trends;
- changes to law, organisational policy or risk.

Record decisions and communicate changes that affect members. Governance should make the community more predictable without making it bureaucratic for its own sake.

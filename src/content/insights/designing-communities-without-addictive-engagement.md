Digital products often treat engagement as an unquestioned good. More visits, longer sessions and more reactions become evidence that the product is working. For a community, those measures can be misleading.

A resident who finds the correct moving procedure in one minute may have a better outcome than someone who scrolls for twenty. A quiet professional group may produce a valuable answer once a week. High activity may reflect confusion, conflict or repeated failure to find information.

## Define a human outcome

Begin with what members should be able to accomplish: understand an update, find guidance, join an event, contribute experience, connect with the right person or receive support.

Measures should follow the outcome. Search failures, unresolved requests, accessibility barriers and repeated questions may be more useful than total impressions. Combine limited quantitative signals with interviews, support themes and moderator judgement.

If a metric does not inform a responsible decision, question why it is collected.

## Replace the endless feed with structure

An undifferentiated feed makes every item compete for immediate attention. A community portal can separate announcements, maintained knowledge, events, discussions and groups while still offering a useful overview.

Structure helps people stop. They can recognise that they have read the important update or found the current document. Completion is a valid design outcome.

Chronology can remain useful, but it should not erase status and authority. A recent member comment should not visually outrank an enduring safety guide merely because it is new.

## Make notifications purposeful

Notifications should correspond to understandable categories and real importance. Give members reasonable control over frequency and channel. Use digests where they reduce interruption.

Avoid artificial urgency, unexplained badges and repeated prompts whose only purpose is return. A notification should say enough for the recipient to judge when attention is needed without exposing private detail on a device screen.

Critical communication requires an operational channel strategy. Do not assume that a push notification or portal inbox is suitable for every emergency or legal notice.

## Avoid public performance pressure

Streaks, leaderboards, follower counts and visible activity scores can turn participation into competition. That may be appropriate in limited, transparent contexts, but it should not be the default for civic, professional, residential or employee communities.

People contribute in different ways. Some read, connect privately, attend occasionally or share specialised knowledge. Public metrics can devalue quieter participation and create pressure where membership is connected to employment, housing or public services.

## Respect departure and quiet

Members should be able to leave optional groups, reduce notifications and understand account departure. Do not make the quiet choice visually inferior or require repeated confirmation designed to change a decision.

Communities also need seasons. An inactive project group may be complete, not failed. Archive it with context rather than keeping it artificially alive.

## Use recommendation carefully

Recommendations can help a member discover a relevant group or document. They can also become opaque systems that maximise consumption and expose sensitive inferences.

Prefer transparent, purpose-limited suggestions: related guidance chosen by editors, groups based on an explicit membership attribute or recent authoritative updates. Explain why something appears and avoid inferring interests unrelated to the community purpose.

## Design for trust, not compulsion

A calm community product can still be engaging in the ordinary sense: useful, welcoming and worth returning to. It simply refuses to treat every spare minute as inventory.

Portal Page's principle is [people before metrics](/principles/#people-before-metrics). That requires product decisions, organisational governance and a commercial model that do not depend on manufacturing attention.

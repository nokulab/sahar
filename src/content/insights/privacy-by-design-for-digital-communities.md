Privacy by design means considering privacy while the community and technology are being shaped, when the most important decisions can still change. It is not a review performed after every field, integration and business incentive has already been fixed.

For digital communities, the approach begins with context. Participation can reveal relationships, interests, locations, work, identity and life circumstances. Even ordinary conversation can become sensitive when moved outside the audience for which it was intended.

## Define the community purpose

A clear purpose limits collection and use. Write a sentence explaining why the community exists and the tasks the portal supports. Then test every proposed data field and feature against it.

“It might be useful later” is not a strong purpose. If the organisation cannot identify who will use an item and for what decision, do not collect it by default.

Purpose also helps members understand the space. They can decide whether a contribution belongs and what audience they reasonably expect.

## Map people and information

List the member groups, administrators, moderators, support staff, suppliers and connected systems. Map the information each actor provides, receives or can access.

Include indirect flows. A notification might reveal a private group name on a device screen. Search might expose a restricted title in a result. An integration error log might copy personal information into a support system. Export may transfer material to administrators who did not previously have access.

The map does not need to begin as a technical diagram. A table of actors, purposes, information and lifecycle is often enough to reveal questions.

## Minimise identity

Private communities need to know who belongs, but they do not always need a detailed public profile. Separate information required for eligibility from information displayed to other members.

Choose privacy-conscious defaults. If a directory is optional, say so. If a real name is required for a professional purpose, explain why. Avoid precise location, demographics or biography fields unless they support a defined task.

Review what happens when a person belongs to several groups. A profile visible in one context should not automatically become visible everywhere.

## Make roles express responsibility

Least privilege is both a security and privacy control. A publisher may not need access to membership records. A group coordinator may only need to manage one group. A moderator may need discussion context but not billing information.

Write down the real responsibilities first, then configure roles. Test joiner, mover and leaver scenarios. Temporary access should expire. Privileged access should be reviewed rather than inherited forever.

Members also need audience cues. Before posting, they should understand whether content is visible to a group, the whole community or administrators.

## Design understandable choices

A privacy choice is meaningful when alternatives are equally visible and consequences are explained at the decision point. Avoid preselected optional sharing, confusing double negatives and buttons designed to make refusal difficult.

Notification settings deserve special care. People may need important organisation information without wanting a prompt for every community interaction. Separate channels and frequencies by purpose where practical.

Leaving should not become a maze. Explain what happens to profile information, past contributions and required records. Some cases need human review, but the route should still be clear.

## Challenge analytics

Begin with the decision, not the tracking tool. If an organisation wants to know whether members can find a guide, task research, search-gap analysis or aggregate page counts may be more useful than individual behavioural histories.

Collect the least detailed measure that can inform the decision. Limit access and retention. Avoid turning community activity into performance monitoring, especially in employment or power-imbalanced contexts.

Raw engagement is not a universal outcome. A quiet, efficient visit may indicate that the portal worked.

## Assess integrations as boundary changes

Every integration expands the system that handles community information. Document the purpose, fields, direction, frequency, authorisation, errors, supplier and exit plan.

Do not connect systems simply because an integration exists. A customer relationship system, identity provider and email platform may each have different information practices. Passing information between them can create a new use that members were never told about.

Minimise at the field level. If a membership check only needs an identifier and status, do not transfer an entire profile.

## Treat AI as a new purpose

Artificial intelligence features should not inherit permission from ordinary service delivery. Summarisation, moderation assistance, recommendations and content generation can involve different information and risks.

Define the purpose. Identify the provider and model. Determine whether prompts or outputs are retained or used for training. Consider false results, bias, explainability and human review. Give customers appropriate controls and explain the capability to members where it affects them.

Portal Page's principle is that private community content should not automatically become training material. AI assistance for moderation still requires documented scope and human accountability.

## Plan retention and deletion

Different information needs different lifecycles. Membership records, discussion content, security logs, support requests and backups should not all inherit one indefinite period.

Define the trigger, period and disposal action. Account for legal holds and required records. Test whether deletion works across active systems and whether backup restoration reintroduces removed information.

Communicate the practical outcome to members. “Delete account” may not mean “delete every contribution”, and the difference should not be a surprise.

## Review change

Privacy by design continues after launch. Review new features, processors, data uses and community purposes before deployment. Revisit inactive fields and groups. Listen to members who experience the consequences differently from administrators.

Use incidents and near misses to improve the system, not merely to assign blame. Keep policies aligned with product behaviour. If the business model changes, reassess the privacy incentives as well as the wording.
